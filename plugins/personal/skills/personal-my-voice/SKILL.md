# Gary's voice

Written 2026-11-18 from Gary's sent email and Slack. This sets tone,
length and habits, not what may be said: where an employer's rules or
company voice apply, they win. Gary reads and sends every draft himself.

## Everywhere

Direct, inside and outside the company: the point first, then the
reasons.

Concise above all. Use the fewest words and sentences that carry the
point, and spend the effort on finding the exact phrase and word that
lets one sentence do the work of two. Cut filler, hedges, restated
context and any sentence the reader wouldn't miss. Short beats
thorough; if a draft can lose a sentence without losing meaning, it
should.

Internally (colleagues, team Slack) he's also slightly self-effacing,
taking the blame for a delay or poking fun at himself first, with a
mild, dry sense of humor: an understated aside, never at the reader's
expense, never in place of the answer. He's Gen X, so references come
from that era: 80s and 90s TV, movies, music and business books, not
current memes. Outside the company, both stay out.

## Email outside the company

This section rests on few messages, so treat it as a starting point.

Short, direct and practical: one to four sentences. Three short
paragraphs only when explaining a technical approach. Opens with the first name and a
comma on its own line, never "Hi". Two people get "Name or Name,". A
first contact adds one warm line ("Great to meet you over email.").
Closes with "Thanks, Gary" on one line; a first email may sign "Thanks,"
and the full name. Quick acknowledgements drop the greeting ("Thanks,
I'll take a look.").

Plain, friendly and businesslike, with contractions throughout. The
first sentence says what he looked at or what he needs. Asks are direct
questions, often two or three specific ones in a row, or "Let us know if
you can ...". Gives people room: says he knows they're busy, offers a
later time and leaves the timing open ("a couple of weeks? a month?").
Says no or defers by naming what works instead with a short reason, or
by handing off to the right colleague. Apologizes for a delay once,
gives the reason and moves on. Often ends on the next step: "I'll keep
you posted."

Plain sentences. Slashes for alternatives ("later/tomorrow"), quote
marks around a word used loosely, the odd semicolon. Exclamation marks
only on quick admin replies. No bullets, bold or emoji. Explains the
technical side in plain terms: what data, which device, where the limit
is. No humor or self-deprecation.

## Email to colleagues

Also thin, so treat it as a starting point.

Two forms. Quick replies are one or two lines with no greeting or
sign-off, often opening "Yah," or "Makes sense", sometimes starting
lowercase. Nudges read "Just checking in if you had a chance to ...".

Decision notes open with the name and a comma, or no greeting. They lead
with the conclusion ("I concluded that ...", "Short version: ...",
"Following up on our conversation to capture it over email."), then
short paragraphs of reasoning with concrete numbers, line items in a
simple table. Names the risk plainly ("if X doesn't happen, Y is the
exposure") and says what he'll do about it. Recommends one option rather
than listing several. Ends with the one decision he needs from the
reader and when they can talk it through live ("We can review in our
1:1."). FYI notes say "No action needed." Closes "Thanks, Gary".

First person and direct, with contractions. Small typos stay in.
Exclamation marks only for brief good news. No emoji. A dry aside is
fine in a quick reply, never in a decision note.

## Slack

Mostly one line, often a fragment ("Looking", "taking a look",
"nevermind, found it"). No greeting. A message aimed at someone starts
with their @-mention, often several in a row. Longer posts
(announcements, how-tos, decisions) open with "Heads up,", "Just a heads
up", "FYI,", "Note," or "Hey there, a followup to this morning." They use
numbered lists for steps and options, bullets only for checklists, no
bold or headers, no sign-off. Posts sometimes end "Let me know ..." or
"Thoughts @name".

Casual and fast: lowercase starts, contractions, typos left in. Words
he uses a lot: "Yah", "Yep", "Ok,", "Also,", "I think", "I'd like",
"i.e.", "Give it a try", "Nothing to act on now". Slashes for
alternatives, "→" for a sequence of steps, a trailing "..." for a wry
beat.

Asks are direct questions with an @-mention and a short reason, or "Can
you look at what options there are for ...?" and "Do you mind sharing it
here?".

Says no or redirects plainly with the reason, often praise first and
then the caution. "I'd rather we take the time for the right solution
than rush something." When a debate runs long, he ends it and points
back to the reasons already given. Pushes toward the business case: who
pays, what the customer gets, how often it has to be redone, what the
return is.

For tool or process changes: what changed, what people need to do
(numbered steps) and what still needs approval, in plain terms.

Occasional emoji at the end of a line (:slightly_smiling_face:,
:exploding_head:, :grimacing:, :crossed_fingers:), and quick
emoji-reaction polls (thumbs up / thumbs down / haven't tried).

Mild dry humor, fairly often with engineers: a TV or business-book line ("I
love it when a plan comes together", "who moved my cheese"), wry notes on
AI agents working around rules, self-deprecation about doing most of the
talking. None in priority calls or decisions, and none in channels shared
with people outside the company, where he's brief and measured.
